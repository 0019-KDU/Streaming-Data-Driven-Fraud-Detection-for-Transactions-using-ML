# Production fraud systems score synchronously, then learn slowly

Real companies build real-time fraud detection the same way. A **synchronous checkpoint** sits inside the payment or user action. It fetches three tiers of features in parallel (request-time, streaming-cached velocity aggregates, batch history), runs rules and a gradient-boosted or deep model side by side, and maps the result to a graduated action: allow, step-up, review, hold, block. All of this happens within a budget of roughly **50–350 ms**. A slower **asynchronous loop** runs around that checkpoint. It handles graph and ring analysis, attack detection, delayed chargeback labels, shadow deployments and retraining. What sets production systems apart is mostly this surrounding discipline rather than the model. Stripe compensates for the labels its own blocks censor by letting a random slice of risky payments through. Nubank runs every new defense in shadow mode first. Stripe and DoorDash gate releases on block rate and false-positive guardrails rather than AUC. Uber and Grab mostly feed graph scores into the main model as features rather than running GNNs inline. Your FYP already has the right skeleton: Kafka, a scorer, a decision engine with APPROVE/REVIEW/HOLD/BLOCK, Redis velocity, MLflow and Airflow. It has three problems. Its scoring path is broken (8 of 88 features reach the model). Its headline numbers conflict and some cannot be reproduced. Its best production ideas sit in a side app that the streaming path never calls. The fastest route to an interview-grade showcase is to subtract before adding. Rotate the leaked Kafka key. Restore one shared feature pipeline. Regenerate one honest out-of-time results table. Wire the decision engine into the live path. Then add two or three cheap, visible production practices: shadow scoring, PSI monitoring and a latency benchmark.

## Every published design converges on checkpoint, feature tiers, rules plus ML, and graduated actions

Company write-ups agree closely on architecture even though their stacks differ. **Stripe Radar assesses "more than 1,000 characteristics" per transaction and decides in under 100 ms.** It blocks only about 0.1% of legitimate payments, at a fraud base rate of about 1 in 1,000 ([Stripe](https://stripe.dev/blog/how-we-built-it-stripe-radar)). Monzo's reactive platform makes the pattern explicit in four steps: choose controls, load features, run controls, apply actions. It splits the work into a Go Engine, a DAG-based Feature Loader and a stateful Action Applier ([Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/build-a-reactive-fraud-prevention-platform)). Nubank's "Flow Orchestrator" routes each event through more than 40 parallel rules and models for PIX transfers. It handles about **450M events per day at >99.98% availability**, and complex flows dropped from 550 ms to about 350 ms after a DAG refactor ([Nubank](https://building.nu.com/scaling-fraud-defense-how-nubank-evolved-its-risk-analysis-platform/)). Uber's 2017 Mastermind does the same thing at marketplace checkpoints. A feature aggregation service fetches real-time and precomputed features in parallel, an ML service adds risk scores as further features, and then **300 rules execute in about 30 ms** ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/mastermind/)).

The synchronous path is not optional for *prevention*. Visa describes fraud scores being used to approve or decline "within milliseconds" during authorization ([Visa](https://www.visa.com/en-us/thought-leadership/risk-fraud/what-is-fraud-scoring)). The ULB Fraud Detection Handbook separates real-time pre-authorization layers from near-real-time scoring that can only block a card for *future* transactions ([Handbook](https://fraud-detection-handbook.github.io/fraud-detection-handbook/Chapter_2_Background/FDS.html)). That split is the one to remember. A Spark micro-batch job that scores after the fact is a legitimate component, but it is a post-authorization monitor, not a gate. Confluent places Spark micro-batch latency "typically in the seconds range" against Flink's sub-second continuous processing ([Confluent](https://www.confluent.io/compare/spark-streaming-vs-flink)). Treat that comparison with care, because Confluent has a commercial interest in Flink. The model itself is rarely the bottleneck. One third-party benchmark puts a 50-tree XGBoost model at about **670 µs per sample in Python and 80–150 µs in ONNX Runtime** ([Timber summary](https://news.lavx.hu/article/timber-aot-compiling-classical-ml-models-to-native-c-for-microsecond-inference)). The p99 budget therefore goes on feature lookups, serialization and tail latency.

Rules and ML always coexist, and the design detail that matters is **separating the detector score from the action policy**. Monzo distinguishes Detectors (ML outputting a fraud type and confidence), Action Controls and an Action Selection Control. Its controls are written as Starlark pure functions so they can be backtested on history ([Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/build-a-reactive-fraud-prevention-platform)). Nubank skips the slower model entirely when a rule confidently flags high risk, and it plans declarative configs so analysts can write rules without engineers ([Nubank](https://building.nu.com/scaling-fraud-defense-how-nubank-evolved-its-risk-analysis-platform/)). Uber's move from Python-dictionary rules to a whitelisted AST language with **Evaluate** (logged, no user impact) and **Active** modes cut rule deployment from about an hour to about a minute. Analysts then wrote 200 rules in three months ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/mastermind/)). DoorDash's 2026 engine similarly decouples rules from app deployments and replays recorded facts to backtest a rule ([DoorDash](https://careersatdoordash.com/?p=15737)); that post was read only via search excerpt. Actions are graduated rather than binary. Airbnb applies targeted friction such as micro-authorizations and A/B-tests each friction's cost to good users ([Airbnb](https://medium.com/airbnb-engineering/fighting-financial-fraud-with-targeted-friction-82d950d8900e)). Its account-takeover model estimates "the probability that each login or action … is being performed by the true account owner" and triggers one-time-code verification on new devices ([Airbnb Newsroom](https://news.airbnb.com/prevent-account-takeovers/)).

## Velocity features live in an online store, while graph intelligence mostly arrives as precomputed features

Velocity and aggregate features are the core of fraud signal. Stripe's examples include the number of countries a card was used in over 24 hours and the card's two most frequent IPs ([Stripe primer](https://stripe.com/guides/primer-on-machine-learning-for-fraud-protection)). Monzo names three freshness tiers. "Just in Time" features come from the payment context. "Near Real Time" features, such as today's total spend, are precomputed asynchronously and cached. "Batch" features are refreshed by SQL. Monzo's Feature Loader returns partial results with per-feature errors and timeouts, so a slow feature degrades gracefully instead of failing the payment ([Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/build-a-reactive-fraud-prevention-platform)). DoorDash serves Flink-computed features from a Redis-backed store ([DoorDash](https://careers.doordash.com/?p=3828)). Coinbase targets **1–2 s freshness and p99 online reads under 100 ms** using Spark Structured Streaming behind Tecton ([Coinbase](https://www.coinbase.com/blog/how-coinbase-builds-sequence-features-for-machine-learning)); that figure comes from a search snippet because the page was blocked.

The hard part is not the online store but **training/serving skew**. Google's Rules of ML says to reuse code between training and serving, because doing so "eliminates a source of training-serving skew". It also says to log the features actually used at serving time and train on them ([Rules of ML](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/guides/rules-of-ml)). Feast builds training sets with point-in-time joins that scan backward from each row's timestamp. It warns that values backfilled later can leak unless a `created_timestamp <= entity_timestamp` filter is applied ([Feast](https://docs.feast.dev/getting-started/concepts/point-in-time-joins)). DoorDash's three-stage "dark shipping" explicitly checks training/inference feature consistency during a 1% shadow phase, before 100% shadow and an A/B test ([ZenML summary of DoorDash](https://www.zenml.io/mlops-database/doordash-doordashs-ml-platform-dark-shipping-rollout-for-ml-fraud-detection-models-with-shadow-traffic-fault-isolation-a)). Coinbase's later move to Spark Real-Time Mode is reported with online/offline consistency "up to 98%" ([Databricks, vendor](https://www.databricks.com/customers/coinbase/lakeflow)). Even a well-resourced team, then, measures parity rather than assuming it.

Graph analysis for fraud rings runs in three tiers, and real-time GNN inference is the exception. In real time, companies do cheap neighborhood lookups on shared entities. Grab's graph platform serves OLTP search on Amazon Neptune for cases such as many accounts sharing one device, with Kafka streaming writes ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/graph-service-platform)). Booking.com generates graphs inside request-time screening ([Booking.com](https://medium.com/booking-com-development/leverage-graph-technology-for-real-time-fraud-detection-and-prevention-438336076ea5)). In batch, GNN scores become features. Uber's relational GCN on a driver–rider graph produced **two score features that gave 15% better precision with minimal false-positive increase**, though Uber stated it was not in production ([Uber](https://eng.uber.com/fraud-detection/)). Wise is building PageRank, community detection and label propagation into a feature store ([Wise](https://wise.jobs/blog/2026-9/keeping-millions-safe-scaling-fincrime-machine-learning-at)). PayPal reportedly needs multi-hop graph queries "within a few milliseconds" ([Aerospike, vendor](https://aerospike.com/blog/where-fraud-detection-fits-on-graph-databases/)). Interview-prep guides likewise put ring analysis "periodically (not real-time)" ([AlgoMaster](https://algomaster.io/learn/ml-system-design/design-fraud-detection-system)). For a student system, a credible graph story is "shared-entity counts online, connected components offline, fed back as features". Vendor claims of real-time GNN detection should be discounted against company practice.

## Labels arrive late, your own blocks censor them, and deployment is gated by guardrails

Fraud labels are chargebacks, and they mature slowly. Visa issuers generally have **120 days** to dispute, extendable to 540 in some cases ([Chargebacks911](https://chargebacks911.com/chargeback-rules/chargeback-time-limit/visa-chargeback-time-limit/)). Stripe historically left about a **60-day buffer** between the newest training data and the present ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/stripe-ml-models-fraud)). The subtler problem is selection bias. A blocked payment never produces an outcome, so a model trained on its predecessor's surviving traffic inherits its blind spots. Stripe's published answer is counterfactual evaluation. It lets a small random share of would-be-blocked payments through (first a uniform 5%, later a propensity curve concentrated near the threshold and funded by a fixed loss budget) and weights each by 1/p. That gives unbiased precision and recall and lets Stripe compare arbitrary thresholds offline ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/stripe-ml-models-fraud); [PyData Seattle 2015](https://pydata.org/seattle2015/schedule/presentation/17/)). Dal Pozzolo et al. show that the alert–feedback loop and verification latency bias the training sample, especially under concept drift ([TNNLS 2017](https://boracchi.faculty.polimi.it/docs/2017_FraudsTNNLS.pdf)). Wise is building a "versioned, auditable ground-truth" label platform for the same reason ([Wise](https://wise.jobs/blog/2026-9/keeping-millions-safe-scaling-fincrime-machine-learning-at)).

Safe deployment is usually shadow first, then a guarded rollout. "All defenses go through a shadow phase first" at Nubank ([Nubank](https://building.nu.com/scaling-fraud-defense-how-nubank-evolved-its-risk-analysis-platform/)), and Monzo uses shadow mode "extensively" with hot-reloaded models from a registry ([Monzo 2022](https://monzo.com/blog/2022/04/26/monzos-machine-learning-stack)). Stripe adds a step many students miss. It matches the new model's score distribution to the old one so that the share of transactions above each merchant's threshold stays stable, then checks false positive rate, block rate and authorization rate per merchant on a subset of users ([Stripe primer](https://stripe.com/guides/primer-on-machine-learning-for-fraud-protection)). A model with better AUC can still silently double a merchant's block rate. Few company sources use the phrase "champion/challenger". The Thoughtworks bank case and DoorDash's Curie A/B stage are the closest equivalents, and both promote the challenger only after a governed or statistically significant win ([Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/en-us/insights/blog/using-cd-machine-learning-models-tackle-fraud); [ZenML/DoorDash](https://www.zenml.io/mlops-database/doordash-doordashs-ml-platform-dark-shipping-rollout-for-ml-fraud-detection-models-with-shadow-traffic-fault-isolation-a)).

Thresholds come from economics, not from F1. Stripe works through an example: a $26 sale at 8% margin earns $2.08. A fraud costs the sale plus a $15 chargeback fee, which equals about 18.71 good sales. Blocking therefore pays off whenever precision at the threshold exceeds **about 5.07%** ([Stripe primer](https://stripe.com/guides/primer-on-machine-learning-for-fraud-protection)). That is why Revolut's press-reported operating point of roughly **30% precision at 96% recall** can be rational, especially when the friction is an in-app confirmation rather than a hard decline ([TechHQ](https://techhq.com/?p=192761)). Capacity also limits thresholds. Investigators can check only a few alerts per day, so the Handbook frames the model's job as precision within an "alert budget" ([Handbook](https://fraud-detection-handbook.github.io/fraud-detection-handbook/Chapter_2_Background/FDS.html)). On monitoring, the PSI bands of <0.1 stable, 0.1–0.25 moderate and >0.25 significant are standard practice ([Arize](https://arize.com/glossary/population-stability-index-psi/)). A Journal of Risk Model Validation paper notes those cut-offs lack statistical grounding ([Risk.net](https://www.risk.net/journal-of-risk-model-validation/7725371/statistical-properties-of-the-population-stability-index)), so present PSI as a heuristic and apply it to scores as well as inputs. Governance is also moving. US regulators replaced SR 11-7 with **SR 26-2 on April 17, 2026**, keeping requirements for documentation, validation and ongoing monitoring of models such as fraud classifiers ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/sr2602.htm)).

## Honest IEEE-CIS evaluation tops out near 0.93–0.95 AUC, and most student mistakes inflate the numbers

The evaluation literature points to one protocol. Split forward in time, leave a gap that mimics label delay, and give early stopping, calibration, threshold selection and final testing each their own later, disjoint slice. The Handbook uses train/delay/test blocks of 7 days each and recommends prequential validation as "the most robust way to perform model selection" ([Handbook Ch.5](https://fraud-detection-handbook.github.io/fraud-detection-handbook/Chapter_5_ModelValidationAndSelection/ValidationStrategies.html)). scikit-learn's own docs say a calibrator must be fit on data disjoint from the classifier's training data ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)). If calibration, threshold and reported metrics all come from one validation set, the precision and recall at the chosen threshold come out optimistic. No study quantifies the size of that bias for fraud specifically, but the methodological argument is settled.

Metric choice matters as much as split choice. At low prevalence, ROC curves spend most of their area at false-positive rates no operations team could run. The Handbook places the practical limit near FPR 0.001–0.01. It also shows rankings flipping: two trees score ROC-AUC 0.788 vs 0.763 but **AP 0.309 vs 0.496** ([Handbook Ch.4](https://fraud-detection-handbook.github.io/fraud-detection-handbook/Chapter_4_PerformanceMetrics/ThresholdFree.html)). It recommends precision@k tied to the daily alert budget and a card-level variant ([Handbook top-k](https://fraud-detection-handbook.github.io/fraud-detection-handbook/Chapter_4_PerformanceMetrics/TopKBased.html)). Saito and Rehmsmeier's PLOS ONE study backs PR curves over ROC for imbalanced data ([PLOS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4349800/)). IEEE-CIS has **590,540 training transactions with about 3.5% fraud** ([FDB](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.14417v3)), so the random AP baseline is about 0.035.

The 1st-place IEEE-CIS solution gives a ceiling. It built UID = card1 + addr1 + floor(day − D1) but kept the UID itself *out* of the model, because about **68.2% of private-test clients never appear in train**. It used UID-level aggregates and frequency encodings instead ([eda-ai-lab repost](https://eda-ai-lab.tistory.com/448)). UID features lifted local AUC from 0.9363 to 0.9472. The winning blend scored **0.9677 public but 0.9459 private**, the private set lying further in the future ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/leveraging-machine-learning-to-detect-fraud-tips-to-developing-a-winning-kaggle-solution)). Single models scored 0.932–0.941 privately. A strong honest single GBDT on an out-of-time split should therefore land around **0.93–0.95 AUC**. A claim above about 0.96 deserves a leakage check: random splits, test-set threshold or early-stopping reuse, or aggregates computed over the whole dataset including future rows. Kaggle-style aggregates over train plus test are transductive and cannot be reproduced online without point-in-time computation.

Two modeling habits deserve retirement. First, SMOTE and other rebalancing give strong GBDTs no discrimination gain once the threshold is tuned ([Elor & Averbuch-Elor](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08528)). In a peer-reviewed JAMIA study, every imbalance correction produced poor calibration with strong overestimation of minority-class risk ([van den Goorbergh et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9382395)). Second, in-sample target or mean encoding leaks the label. CatBoost's paper names this "prediction shift" and fixes it with ordered statistics computed from preceding rows only ([Prokhorenkova et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09516)). The third trap applies directly to your project: an API that sends about 15 raw fields to a model trained on hundreds. Rules of ML #37 says training and serving should "give exactly the same result" for the same example ([Rules of ML](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/guides/rules-of-ml)). When most inputs default to NaN, trees follow rarely-trained branches, and offline AUC stops describing the deployed system. No published figure quantifies the IEEE-CIS drop with only about 15 fields, so you have to measure it yourself.

## Open-source references are partial, and practitioners reward operating points over AUC

No single public repo covers the whole loop. The Le Borgne handbook (760 stars) is the evaluation reference ([GitHub](https://www.github.com/Fraud-Detection-Handbook/fraud-detection-handbook)). Ververica's Flink demo (351 stars, last pushed 2023) shows dynamic JSON rules over Kafka control topics with no ML ([Flink blog](https://flink.apache.org/2020/01/15/advanced-flink-application-patterns-vol.1-case-study-of-a-fraud-detection-system/)). Google's fraudfinder (252 stars) walks through a feature store, training, endpoint and monitoring as numbered notebooks ([fraudfinder](https://github.com/GoogleCloudPlatform/fraudfinder)). The NVIDIA blueprint covers GNN training plus Triton inference with Shapley explanations, but needs GPUs and an NGC key ([NVIDIA](https://github.com/NVIDIA-AI-Blueprints/financial-fraud-detection)). AWS's GNN blueprint is archived ([GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/awslabs/realtime-fraud-detection-with-gnn-on-dgl)). A student project that genuinely ties streaming, decisioning, serving and honest evaluation together would therefore stand out, but only if it works and its numbers hold up.

Practitioner voices are anecdotal but consistent. One fintech ML hiring manager on Hacker News set the bar as "a fraud model that requires four 9's, 100ms latency, with 50,000 calls an hour, 80% recall and 50% precision" ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42141546)). The bar is stated as an operating point plus an SLA, with no AUC in it. A former banking-fraud practitioner described gains coming from "tying together every piece of knowledge… across all databases" and building "massive decision trees", with false positives staying high because fraud is rare ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=35055534)). The 388-comment "Automatic fraud detection is making my life hell" thread shows the customer cost of false positives ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=38038713)). Reddit threads could not be read directly; a mirrored r/datascience snippet describes fraud work as "a lot of business knowledge and building decision trees for different fraud schemes uncovered by investigators" ([r/datascience mirror](https://axebps-redlib.hf.space/r/datascience/comments/1jdotmw/what_is_financial_fraud_prevention_data_science/)). Portfolio advice, which mostly comes from vendors with a commercial interest, converges on a few points. Build a pipeline rather than a notebook. Justify the metric. Give one-command startup, an architecture diagram and a limitations section ([Udacity](https://www.udacity.com/blog/10-machine-learning-projects-that-will-boost-your-portfolio/); [Springboard](https://www.springboard.com/blog/data-science/machine-learning-portfolio/)). The standard interview prompt "design a real-time fraud system" expects these components: a latency budget, shared offline and online feature definitions, layered rules-then-model-then-review scoring, label delay, cost-based thresholds, offline graph analysis, champion/challenger and drift monitoring ([AlgoMaster](https://algomaster.io/learn/ml-system-design/design-fraud-detection-system)). Your repo can answer that prompt section by section if the pieces are wired together.

### Tier 2 — production practices to adopt or demonstrate

Pick these in order. The first four change what the project *is*. The rest are cheap demonstrations that map onto interview questions.

**1. Honest out-of-time evaluation (highest value, about 1–2 days).** Split IEEE-CIS by `TransactionDT` into train (roughly months 1–4), early-stopping, calibration+threshold, and a final untouched test month. Leave a gap of about 1 week before the test month, following the Handbook's delay block ([Handbook Ch.5](https://fraud-detection-handbook.github.io/fraud-detection-handbook/Chapter_5_ModelValidationAndSelection/ValidationStrategies.html)). Fit `CalibratedClassifierCV(cv='prefit')` on the calibration slice only, choose the threshold on that slice, and report on test only ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)). Compute UID and mean or frequency encodings from past rows only (expanding windows), not over the whole dataset. Drop SMOTE ([van den Goorbergh et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9382395)). Report AUC-ROC for comparability, plus **AP** (against the about-0.035 baseline), **recall at FPR 0.1%/0.5%/1%**, **precision@k** for a stated daily review budget ([Handbook top-k](https://fraud-detection-handbook.github.io/fraud-detection-handbook/Chapter_4_PerformanceMetrics/TopKBased.html)), and **dollar-weighted recall** (the share of fraudulent `TransactionAmt` caught). Include a baseline row, such as logistic regression or a three-rule rulebook, so the GBDT's gain is visible. Expect AUC around 0.92–0.95. Present that figure confidently, because it is the realistic range.

**2. Close the train/serve gap honestly (about 1–2 days).** There are two complementary moves. First, a *replay test*: push out-of-time test rows through the real serving path, carrying the full raw payload, and report AUC/AP there. This proves the deployed pipeline reproduces offline scores. Second, a *decision-time model* trained only on fields the producer actually sends, plus velocity aggregates computed point-in-time from Redis-equivalent logic. Report its metrics next to the full model. The gap between the two numbers is the honest cost of feature availability, which Rules of ML says should be zero for the same inputs ([Rules of ML](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/guides/rules-of-ml)). This explains the ~0.02 probabilities far better than a scripted viva answer. Re-enable velocity features in training only if the offline computation uses identical windows and inclusive/exclusive semantics.

**3. Make the synchronous and asynchronous paths explicit (about 1 day).** Promote `api_direct.py` to the *authorization path*: FastAPI → shared feature pipeline → Redis velocity → model → decision engine (APPROVE/REVIEW/HOLD/BLOCK with reason codes). Document the Spark job as the *post-authorization monitor* that writes to the prediction topics, dashboard and alerts. That mirrors the Handbook's real-time versus near-real-time split ([Handbook](https://fraud-detection-handbook.github.io/fraud-detection-handbook/Chapter_2_Background/FDS.html)). In Spark, replace row-by-row scoring inside `mapInPandas` with one vectorised `predict_proba` per pandas batch, load the model once per executor, and remeasure the 17.8 s/transaction figure. Have both paths call the same decision-engine module so rules and ML are layered identically. That is Monzo's detector-versus-action separation ([Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/build-a-reactive-fraud-prevention-platform)).

**4. Latency benchmark (about half a day).** Run a small load-generator script against the sync endpoint and report p50/p95/p99 and throughput on stated hardware. Split out model time from feature-lookup time, since the model should take under about 1 ms ([Timber summary](https://news.lavx.hu/article/timber-aot-compiling-classical-ml-models-to-native-c-for-microsecond-inference)). Report Spark end-to-end latency separately and state it is bounded below by the 10-second trigger. Compare against Stripe's <100 ms ([Stripe](https://stripe.dev/blog/how-we-built-it-stripe-radar)) without claiming parity.

**5. Cost-based thresholds (about half a day).** Replace the F1-optimal threshold with Stripe's break-even logic. Choose an assumed margin, chargeback fee and review cost, derive the break-even precision, and choose the BLOCK threshold and REVIEW band on the calibration slice ([Stripe primer](https://stripe.com/guides/primer-on-machine-learning-for-fraud-protection)). Cap REVIEW volume at a stated analyst budget. Show net savings on the test month.

**6. Shadow / champion-challenger (about half a day).** Load a second MLflow model version, for example LightGBM, in the Spark job. Score every event with both models, act only on the champion, and write the challenger's score to a `shadow_predictions` topic. Add a dashboard panel comparing block rate, score distribution and agreement. That is Nubank's "shadow phase first" and Stripe's block-rate guardrail in miniature ([Nubank](https://building.nu.com/scaling-fraud-defense-how-nubank-evolved-its-risk-analysis-platform/)).

**7. Monitoring with PSI (about half a day).** Compute PSI on the live score distribution and the top 5–10 features against the training reference, over a rolling window. Show it on the dashboard with the 0.1/0.25 bands, and label them as heuristics ([Arize](https://arize.com/glossary/population-stability-index-psi/); [Risk.net](https://www.risk.net/journal-of-risk-model-validation/7725371/statistical-properties-of-the-population-stability-index)).

**8. Label delay (design doc plus small simulation, about half a day).** IEEE-CIS gives every label immediately, which no production system has. Simulate maturation by training only on data older than N days at each retrain, and show the metric cost. In the README, describe how blocked transactions would be evaluated with a randomized allow-slice and inverse-propensity weighting ([InfoQ/Stripe](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/stripe-ml-models-fraud)). Do not build it.

**9. Optional if time remains.** Add a "shared-entity" feature (the count of distinct `card1` per `P_emaildomain`/`addr1`/device in Redis) as a minimal graph signal, with batch connected components explained as future work ([Uber](https://eng.uber.com/fraud-detection/); [Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/graph-service-platform)). Log the served feature vector per decision to Kafka so you can claim "log-and-train" readiness. Add a GitHub Actions workflow running the parity and unit tests. Split the ~3,000-line DAG into `features/`, `training/` and `evaluation/` modules with a thin Airflow wrapper.

Leave out Flink rewrites, a real feature store such as Feast or Tecton, GNNs and Kubernetes. Each would cost weeks and replace a working story with an unfinished one. Name them in a "what I'd do at scale" section instead.

### Tier 3 — how to talk about it in interviews

Lead with the architecture and the trade-offs, not the AUC. A strong 60-second framing runs like this: "Kafka ingestion; a synchronous FastAPI scorer with Redis velocity features and a layered rules-plus-ML decision engine for the authorization path; a Spark Structured Streaming monitor for post-authorization alerting; Airflow, MLflow and MinIO for retraining; out-of-time evaluation reporting AP and recall at fixed FPR." Then volunteer your limitations before you are asked, because hiring managers in this space test for exactly them ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42141546)). First, Spark micro-batch is not an authorization gate. You chose it because it was what you knew, and production systems use a synchronous service or Flink ([Confluent](https://www.confluent.io/compare/spark-streaming-vs-flink)). Second, IEEE-CIS's anonymized V/C/D columns do not exist at request time, so you measured the gap between the full model and the decision-time model rather than hiding it. Third, the dataset's labels are immediate, so you simulated label maturation and explained IPW for censored blocks. Fourth, the hardest bug you hit was train/serve skew from deleted feature code, and you fixed it with a single shared module plus a parity test. That last point is a real production failure mode, described in Google's own guidance, and you found and fixed it yourself.

Two more habits help. Quote numbers only from the regenerated `metrics.json`, each with its split and hardware. When asked "why XGBoost?", cite the evidence: GBDTs remain common in fraud tooling (Block's postings name XGBoost and LightGBM ([Block](https://block.xyz/careers/jobs/4680661008))), while Stripe moved to DNNs only at a scale where it had to replace XGBoost's "memorization" ([Stripe](https://stripe.dev/blog/how-we-built-it-stripe-radar)). Keep the thesis's LightGBM result and the repo's XGBoost result reconciled in one sentence. For example: "the thesis compared models under protocol X; the repo now uses protocol Y, and here is the table." Without that sentence, the discrepancy reads as carelessness.

## Conclusion

The research changes what "production-grade" should mean for this project. Streaming technology is not the differentiator. Every company here uses Kafka-class ingestion, and none of the reference repos is complete. What separates production systems is three disciplines student projects usually skip: one feature definition shared between training and serving, evaluation that respects time and censored labels, and decisions driven by cost and guardrails rather than F1. Your repo's most embarrassing failure, deleted feature code that silently produced 8 of 88 features, is exactly the failure those disciplines exist to prevent. That makes it your strongest interview asset once it is fixed and tested.

The practical implication is that you gain the most by restoring correctness and honesty before adding anything new. A working demo with one regenerated out-of-time metrics table (an AUC in the low 0.9s, with AP and recall at fixed FPR beside it), a measured p99, a shadow model and a PSI panel will outperform a README claiming 0.976 AUC, 500 TPS and 43,378% ROI. Practitioners screen for operating points, latency and candour about limits. They do not screen for leaderboard numbers.
